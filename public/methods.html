Kisan AI — Methods

How the data, models and uncertainty work

← Back to the app

Kisan AI turns NASA Earth observations into a few plain decisions for a small farmer: water this week or not, is my crop stressed, and which crop rotation fits my land, water and goals. This page explains every step so an extension worker or judge can check it. Everything marked live runs in the deployed app today; designed is the next step, not yet built.

2. Harmonising different resolutions

The datasets range from 30 m to 300 km and from half-hourly to static, so the app puts them on two common analysis grids and keeps a record of the original resolution so it can say how sure it is.

Time: one calendar-month grid per field live

  • Daily → month: rain and ET are summed; temperature and soil wetness are averaged; heat days (Tmax ≥ 38 °C) and frost nights (Tmin ≤ 2 °C) are counted.
  • 8-day NDVI → month: clear-sky composites in the month are averaged; the number of images is kept (fewer images → lower confidence).
  • Normals: each month gets its 30-year POWER normal; part months compare against the normal for the same number of days, so a 10-day month is not called "dry".
  • Derived: water balance = rain − ET; soil-wetness anomaly = month mean − normal; rain anomaly % = (rain − normal)/normal.

Space: the MODIS 250 m sinusoidal grid live

  • The field's MODIS tile and pixel are computed from latitude/longitude (sinusoidal projection), and a 7×7 window (~1.6 km) is read straight from cloud-optimised GeoTIFFs — no full image download.
  • Finer data → 250 m: the 90 m DEM is sampled at each 250 m cell centre and slope comes from central differences between neighbouring cells. HLS 30 m imagery would be aggregated (mean NDVI of the ~70 pixels in each cell). designed
  • Coarser data → 250 m: SMAP (9 km), IMERG (10 km), POWER (~50 km) are assigned to every 250 m cell inside them (nearest-parent resampling). This does not invent detail: all 49 cells share one value, and the confidence notes say so.
  • ECOSTRESS (70 m, irregular overpasses) would be aggregated per cell and per month, keeping only clear, daytime scenes; GEDI (1 km gridded) is assigned like the other coarse layers. designed

4. Rotation strategy scoring

Each candidate crop is first scored for one season (0–100): temperature fit (40), water fit (40: 70 % of seasonal rain + monsoon soil-water carry-over for Rabi, scaled by soil texture + irrigation), and value (20), minus penalties for heat above the crop's limit, frost, soil pH outside its range and — if the farmer has little labour — hand-labour-heavy crops. All Kharif → Rabi → Zaid combinations of the top candidates are then compared on eight criteria (0–100, higher is better):

CriterionHow it is computed
Nutrients (N)+ for legumes, + legume before a heavy feeder, − two heavy feeders in a row (including into next year's Kharif)
Erosion protectionΣ season rain share × (1 − ground cover); extra risk for digging harvests (potato, onion, groundnut); − for field slope > 2 % from the DEM
Organic matterCrop residue (0–3 per crop), + green manure, + deep roots
Pest-cycle break− same crop family back-to-back, + three or more families, + a fallow break
Water saving100 − irrigation needed (mm) / 15
FodderStraw/stover/haulm value of the crops
IncomeΣ today's mandi modal price × typical yield (₹/acre; 2.5 lakh = 100) when all crops are priced, otherwise a relative value index
Drought safetyMean drought tolerance of the crops

The overall score weights these by the farmer's priority (balanced, income, soil health, save water), adds fodder when the farmer needs it, and raises the weight of drought safety when the rainfall slider is set below normal. The three best plans that differ in at least two seasons are shown side by side, with plain reasons and warnings.

5. Uncertainty and how it is shown

  • Scale: one MODIS pixel is ~6 ha and POWER cells are ~50 km — they can mix a field with its neighbours. Every timeline shows this.
  • Clouds: only "good" or "marginal" MODIS pixels are used. Few clear images in the last 7 weeks, or gaps over 5 weeks, lower the confidence level (high / medium / low), and the reasons are listed.
  • Archive gaps: if no past-year image exists, the anomaly is skipped and confidence drops.
  • Models, not measurements: soil moisture and ET are model estimates (MERRA-2, Open-Meteo). SoilGrids is a prediction map; a soil test is better.
  • Prices and yields: mandi prices are one day's reports; yields are typical figures. Income is a rough gross estimate, costs excluded.
  • Data-entry errors: mandi reports more than 4× away from the median are dropped; milled rice is separated from paddy.
  • Each data source fails on its own: the page says which source is unavailable and shows the rest.

6. Where local expertise must validate

  • Stress vs harvest vs pests: the satellite sees greenness, not causes. A field visit decides.
  • Crop parameters: temperature, water, pH and yield values are rounded FAO/ICAR figures; KVK scientists should tune them per agro-climatic zone and variety.
  • Thresholds: NDVI cut-offs and stress rules should be checked against farmer records or crop-cutting surveys before wide use.
  • Translations: Tamil, Kannada, Malayalam, Telugu (and Hindi) text needs review by native-speaking extension workers.
  • Markets and inputs: seed availability, labour, credit and buyers are local knowledge the tool does not have.

7. Farmer-centred design

  • Six interface languages, nine for the assistant; voice input and read-aloud.
  • Every number has a one-line meaning; the Learn tab explains ET, soil-moisture anomaly, NDVI, frost and water balance.
  • Farmers enter their own constraints: water available, last crop, priority, little labour/no machines, fodder needs, crops to avoid, and "what if" rainfall/temperature.
  • Repeated reminders that the tool supports — not replaces — local expertise and the farmer's own observation.

8. Next steps
